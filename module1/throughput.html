Throughput

The data rate a network actually delivers, and what holds it back.

1. What throughput is

Throughput is the actual rate at which data is successfully transferred across a network. It shows what users really experience, not what the connection could do on paper.

An Internet plan may be 100 Mbps, but a download might run at 72 Mbps. That 72 Mbps is the throughput at that moment. It is measured in the same units as bandwidth: bps, Kbps, Mbps and Gbps.

Throughput is the rate of data that actually arrives successfully. It changes from moment to moment.

2. Throughput and data rate

The data rate is what a link is built or set to support. Ethernet might run at 1 Gbps. Throughput is what gets delivered after real conditions take their share.

Link data rate: 1 Gbps minus conditions and protocol overhead Throughput: about 850 Mbps

Not every bit sent is your data. Headers, acknowledgements and control messages are needed for communication but they use up part of the capacity.

3. What lowers throughput

FactorWhy it lowers throughput
CongestionMany devices send at the same time and share the capacity
HardwareA slow network card, switch, router or access point limits the path
Wireless interferenceOther signals, walls and distance disturb the connection
DistanceSignals weaken, especially wireless and some cables
Protocol overheadHeaders and control data use capacity that is not your data
Errors and retransmissionsLost or damaged data must be sent again

4. A worked example

A student downloads an 800 MB file. The plan is 100 Mbps, but with moderate congestion and weak Wi-Fi the measured throughput is 80 Mbps. Throughput is in bits and the file is in bytes, so convert first.

800 MB x 8 = 6,400 megabits 6,400 Mb / 80 Mbps = 80 seconds

That is about 80 seconds, compared with 64 seconds at the full 100 Mbps. A lower rate than advertised does not always mean the provider is at fault. Many things between your device and the server affect it, and the rate also changes during the download.

5. Throughput and bandwidth

BandwidthThroughput
MeaningCapacity of the connectionData successfully delivered
Think of it asLanes on a roadVehicles actually moving
ValueSet by the technology or planVaries with conditions

Bandwidth is how much the connection can carry. Throughput is how much it is carrying.

6. Other measurements

High throughput does not mean a network is healthy. Two other measurements matter too.

MeasureWhat it tells you
LatencyHow long data takes to travel between two points
Packet lossHow many packets never arrive

A video call needs low latency and little packet loss as well as enough throughput.

7. Improving throughput

  • Upgrade slow hardware.
  • Increase the capacity of important links.
  • Reduce unnecessary traffic.
  • Improve wireless coverage and reduce interference.
  • Replace damaged or unsuitable cables.
  • Find and remove bottlenecks.

Finding a bottleneck

A bottleneck is the part of the path that limits the whole path. Here the 100 Mbps link slows everything, even though the first link is much faster.

8. Quick check

What does throughput measure?

The actual amount of data successfully transferred over time.

A link has 100 Mbps bandwidth and carries data at 70 Mbps. What is the throughput?

70 Mbps.

Name three things that can reduce throughput.

For example congestion, wireless interference, slow hardware, protocol overhead or retransmissions.

How long does an 800 MB file take at a steady 80 Mbps?

800 x 8 = 6,400 megabits, divided by 80 gives 80 seconds.

What is a bottleneck?

The slowest part of a path, which limits the speed of the whole path.
