Copper Cables

Understanding how copper cables carry data using electrical signals and the different types of copper media used in networking.

Introduction to Copper Media

Copper is one of the most widely used materials for wired network connections. Copper cables contain metal conductors that carry electrical signals representing network data.

Copper cables are commonly used in local area networks, especially for connecting computers, switches, routers, access points, printers, and other network devices.

Remember

Copper network media carries data using electrical signals.

Types of Copper Cables

There are different types of copper cables. Two important examples are coaxial cable and twisted-pair cable.

Coaxial Cable

A cable with a central conductor surrounded by insulation and shielding.

Twisted-Pair Cable

A cable made from pairs of insulated copper wires twisted together to help reduce interference.

Coaxial Cable

Coaxial cable, often called coax, is a type of copper cable with a central conductor surrounded by insulating material and shielding.

Basic structure of a coaxial cable.

Structure of a Coaxial Cable

A coaxial cable generally contains several layers.

  1. Central conductor: Carries the electrical signal.
  2. Insulator: Separates the central conductor from the shield.
  3. Shield: Helps protect the signal from external interference.
  4. Outer jacket: Protects the cable from physical damage.

Uses of Coaxial Cable

Coaxial cable has been used in several communication applications, including cable television and broadband Internet connections. It was also used in older Ethernet technologies.

Modern Ethernet LANs, however, commonly use twisted-pair Ethernet cabling instead of traditional coaxial Ethernet.

Twisted-Pair Cable

Twisted-pair cable consists of pairs of insulated copper wires that are twisted around each other.

The twisting is important because it helps reduce interference and unwanted electrical effects between the wires.

A twisted-pair cable contains multiple pairs of twisted copper conductors.

Why Are the Wires Twisted?

Electrical signals travelling through copper can be affected by unwanted electromagnetic energy. Twisting the wires in pairs helps reduce the effects of interference and crosstalk.

The twisting also helps ensure that the conductors in a pair experience similar interference, which helps the receiving equipment distinguish the intended signal.

UTP Cable

UTP stands for Unshielded Twisted Pair.

UTP is one of the most common cable types used for Ethernet networks.

A typical Ethernet twisted-pair cable contains four twisted pairs, giving a total of eight conductors.

UTP in Simple Terms

UTP is a copper cable containing four pairs of twisted wires without an additional overall metal shield.

Why Is UTP Common?

  • It is relatively inexpensive.
  • It is flexible and easy to work with.
  • It is widely used for Ethernet LAN connections.
  • It is available in different cable categories for different network requirements.

UTP and Interference

Because UTP does not have an overall shield, it relies on the twisting of its wire pairs and proper installation to reduce unwanted interference.

Environments with particularly high levels of electrical interference may require other cabling solutions, including shielded copper or fiber-optic media.

Ethernet Cable

The term Ethernet cable is commonly used for the copper twisted-pair cables used to connect devices in Ethernet networks.

For example, a computer can be connected to a network switch using an Ethernet cable.

PC → Ethernet cable → Switch

The copper conductors inside the cable carry electrical signals between the devices.

RJ45 Connector

Ethernet twisted-pair cables commonly use an 8P8C modular connector, which is commonly referred to as an RJ45 connector.

An Ethernet modular connector commonly called RJ45.

The connector has eight contact positions that correspond to the eight conductors in a four-pair Ethernet cable.

The conductors must be arranged correctly before the connector is attached to the cable.

Important Distinction

UTP describes the cable.

RJ45 is the common name used for the connector used with Ethernet twisted-pair cabling.

The Eight Conductors

A typical four-pair Ethernet cable contains eight individual conductors.

These conductors are arranged into four pairs:

  1. Blue pair
  2. Orange pair
  3. Green pair
  4. Brown pair

Each pair consists of two wires. This gives us:

4 pairs × 2 wires = 8 conductors

The exact order in which these conductors are placed into the connector is determined by the wiring standard being used.

Ethernet Pinouts

A pinout describes which conductor is connected to each pin position of the connector.

Ethernet twisted-pair cabling commonly uses the T568A and T568B wiring standards.

Comparison of the T568A and T568B conductor arrangements.

T568A

T568A defines a specific order for the eight conductors in an Ethernet connector.

T568B

T568B defines another recognized conductor arrangement. T568A and T568B mainly differ in the positions of the orange and green pairs.

Important

T568A and T568B are wiring arrangements. They are not different types of Ethernet speed or cable.

Straight-Through and Crossover Cables

The wiring arrangement at both ends of an Ethernet cable affects how the cable is constructed.

Straight-Through Cable

A straight-through cable uses the same wiring standard at both ends.

For example:

T568B → T568B

or

T568A → T568A

Crossover Cable

A traditional crossover cable uses different wiring arrangements at its two ends, such as T568A at one end and T568B at the other.

Older Ethernet equipment could require crossover cables when connecting certain types of devices directly. Modern network interfaces often support Auto-MDI/MDI-X, which allows them to automatically adapt to the cable type.

Do Not Memorize This as a Rule for Every Modern Network

Modern Ethernet equipment commonly handles cable differences automatically. The important concept for this module is understanding how the wiring standards determine the conductor arrangement.

Copper and Electromagnetic Interference

Copper carries electrical signals, so the signals can be affected by electromagnetic interference (EMI).

EMI can come from sources such as electrical equipment, motors, power cables, and other sources of electromagnetic energy.

Proper cable design, twisting, installation practices, and in some situations shielding can help reduce interference.

Fiber-optic cable provides another solution because the information travels as light rather than as an electrical signal.

Cable Distance

The maximum usable distance depends on the specific Ethernet standard, cable category, equipment, and installation. Therefore, we should not assume that every Ethernet cable has exactly the same distance limit.

As one common example, Cisco documentation specifies 100 metres for a 100BASE-TX copper segment under the stated conditions.

For longer network links, fiber-optic media may be selected when the required distance and network design call for it.

Advantages of Copper

  • Commonly available.
  • Relatively affordable.
  • Easy to install compared with some other media.
  • Flexible and suitable for many LAN connections.
  • Widely supported by Ethernet networking equipment.

Limitations of Copper

  • Electrical signals can be affected by electromagnetic interference.
  • Copper Ethernet links have distance limitations that depend on the Ethernet standard and cable.
  • Copper cabling can be less suitable than fiber for some high-distance or high-interference environments.

Key Terms

  • Copper media: Network media that carries data using electrical signals.
  • Coaxial cable: A copper cable with a central conductor, insulation, shielding, and an outer jacket.
  • Twisted pair: Two insulated copper conductors twisted together.
  • UTP: Unshielded Twisted Pair.
  • RJ45: Common name for the modular Ethernet connector used with twisted-pair cabling.
  • Pinout: The arrangement of conductors or signals at connector pins.
  • T568A: A standardized conductor arrangement for twisted-pair cabling.
  • T568B: Another standardized conductor arrangement for twisted-pair cabling.
  • EMI: Electromagnetic interference that can affect electrical signals.

Summary

Copper is an important network medium that carries data using electrical signals. Coaxial and twisted-pair cables are examples of copper media.

UTP is widely used for Ethernet LANs. It contains four twisted pairs, giving eight conductors in a typical cable. The conductors are terminated according to recognized wiring arrangements such as T568A or T568B.

The twisting of the pairs helps reduce interference, while proper termination ensures that the conductors are connected correctly.

Copper → electrical signals → twisted pairs → Ethernet connections
