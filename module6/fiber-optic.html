MODULE 6

Fiber-Optic Media

Understanding how fiber-optic cables use light to transmit data and why fiber is important for high-speed and long-distance networking.

Introduction to Fiber-Optic Media

Fiber-optic cable is a type of network media that uses light to transmit data.

Unlike copper cable, which carries electrical signals, fiber-optic cable carries optical signals through very thin strands of glass or plastic.

Fiber is widely used in modern networks because it can support high bandwidth and long-distance communication. It is also immune to electromagnetic interference along the fiber itself.

Remember

Fiber-optic media → data travels as light.

How Does Fiber-Optic Cable Work?

When data needs to be transmitted through a fiber link, networking equipment converts the electrical representation of the data into an optical signal.

A transmitter then sends pulses of light into the fiber. The light travels through the fiber until it reaches the receiving equipment.

At the receiving end, the optical signal is detected and converted back into an electrical signal that networking equipment can process.

Data can be represented by pulses of light travelling through an optical fiber.

Electrical data → light → fiber → light detected → electrical data

Structure of a Fiber-Optic Cable

A fiber-optic cable is made up of several layers that protect the optical fiber and help it transmit light effectively.

Basic structure of a fiber-optic cable.

1. Core

The core is the central part of the optical fiber. Light travels through the core.

2. Cladding

The cladding surrounds the core and has a different refractive index. This allows light to remain guided within the core through a phenomenon called total internal reflection.

3. Coating

A protective coating surrounds the glass fiber and helps protect it from physical damage and environmental effects.

4. Outer Jacket

The outer jacket provides additional physical protection to the cable.

Total Internal Reflection

One important principle behind fiber-optic communication is total internal reflection.

The core and cladding are made with different optical properties. When light travels through the core under the appropriate conditions, it reflects internally rather than escaping through the cladding.

This allows the light signal to continue travelling along the fiber.

You do not need to calculate the physics behind total internal reflection for this module. The important idea is simply that the structure of the fiber helps guide light through the cable.

Fiber and Electromagnetic Interference

One of the major advantages of fiber is its resistance to electromagnetic interference.

Copper cables carry electrical signals, so electromagnetic energy from nearby electrical equipment can interfere with those signals.

Fiber carries information using light rather than electrical current through a metal conductor. Therefore, the optical fiber itself is not affected by electromagnetic interference in the same way copper conductors are.

Fiber → light → no electrical conductor carrying the data → immunity to EMI along the fiber

Fiber for Long-Distance Communication

Fiber-optic technology is commonly used for long-distance communication because optical links can carry data over much longer distances than many common copper Ethernet connections.

The actual distance supported depends on the type of fiber, optical transceivers, wavelength, Ethernet or optical standard, and other characteristics of the network.

Fiber is therefore commonly found in network backbones, connections between buildings, data centers, Internet infrastructure, and service-provider networks.

Important

Do not memorize one distance as the limit for all fiber. Different fiber types and optical systems support different distances.

Single-Mode Fiber

Single-mode fiber (SMF) has a very small core and is designed to allow one primary mode of light to propagate through the fiber.

Because of its characteristics, single-mode fiber is commonly used for long-distance and high-capacity communication.

It is commonly encountered in service-provider networks and connections where long reach is required.

Multimode Fiber

Multimode fiber (MMF) has a larger core and allows multiple modes of light to propagate through the fiber.

Multimode fiber is commonly used for shorter-distance connections such as some data-center and campus-network links.

Because multiple paths of light can travel through the fiber, the characteristics of multimode fiber differ from those of single-mode fiber.

Simple Difference

Single-mode: one primary mode of light, commonly used for longer distances.

Multimode: multiple modes of light, commonly used for shorter-distance links.

Fiber vs Copper

Fiber and copper can both be used to build network connections, but they have different characteristics.

General comparison of fiber-optic and copper network media.

Feature Copper Fiber
Signal Electrical Light
Conductor Metal Glass or plastic fiber
EMI Can be affected Immune along the fiber
Distance Limited by the particular Ethernet standard Can support long-distance links
Bandwidth potential High, depending on the technology Very high
Installation Generally simpler Requires more specialized handling and equipment
Common applications LAN and device connections Backbones, data centers, long-distance links

Advantages of Fiber-Optic Cable

  • Supports high bandwidth.
  • Suitable for long-distance communication.
  • Immune to electromagnetic interference along the fiber.
  • Does not carry the network signal as an electrical current through a metal conductor.
  • Useful for connections between buildings and network infrastructure.
  • Can support very high data rates with appropriate equipment.

Limitations of Fiber-Optic Cable

  • Fiber equipment and installation can cost more than simple copper Ethernet connections.
  • Installation and termination can require specialized tools and skills.
  • The fiber itself must be handled carefully to avoid damage.
  • The correct fiber type and optical equipment must be selected for the required distance and network standard.

Where Is Fiber Used?

Fiber can be found in many parts of modern communication infrastructure.

  • Internet service-provider networks
  • Network backbones
  • Connections between buildings
  • Data centers
  • Campus networks
  • Long-distance communication links
  • Fiber-to-the-home broadband networks

When Would a Network Engineer Choose Fiber?

Fiber may be considered when the network requires one or more of the following:

  • Long transmission distances.
  • High bandwidth.
  • Resistance to electromagnetic interference.
  • High-capacity backbone connections.
  • Connections between buildings.

The final decision still depends on the specific network design, equipment, budget, and required performance.

Key Terms

  • Fiber-optic cable: A network medium that carries information using light.
  • Core: The central part of an optical fiber through which light travels.
  • Cladding: The material surrounding the core that helps guide light within the fiber.
  • Total internal reflection: A phenomenon that helps keep light travelling within the fiber core.
  • Single-mode fiber: Fiber designed to support one primary mode of light.
  • Multimode fiber: Fiber that allows multiple modes of light to propagate.
  • Optical signal: Information represented using light.
  • EMI: Electromagnetic interference.

Summary

Fiber-optic cable is a network medium that carries data using pulses of light through glass or plastic optical fiber.

Its structure includes a core and cladding that guide the light, together with protective layers around the fiber.

Fiber provides important advantages such as high bandwidth, long-distance capability, and immunity to electromagnetic interference along the fiber.

The two major categories discussed here are single-mode and multimode fiber. The appropriate choice depends on the network's distance, capacity, equipment, and other requirements.

Fiber → light → high bandwidth → long-distance communication → EMI immunity

Putting Module 6 Together

We can now connect the three major network media types:

Wireless

Data travels through electromagnetic waves.

Copper

Data travels through electrical signals in metal conductors.

Fiber

Data travels as light through optical fiber.

A network engineer chooses between these media according to factors such as distance, bandwidth, cost, environment, interference, reliability, and installation requirements.
